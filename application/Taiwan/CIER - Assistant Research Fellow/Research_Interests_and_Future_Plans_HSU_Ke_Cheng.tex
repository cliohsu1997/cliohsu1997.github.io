Ke-Cheng Hsu (Clio)\\

I am an empirical industrial organization researcher. My agenda is digital platforms and the design of marketplace rules, and the translation of those rules into economic policy analysis. A platform chooses the refund menu, the fee, and the constraints on price. Each choice changes who buys, who returns, how sellers price, and who bears the cost. I estimate structural models, in Python, R, and C++, that rank such rules by welfare, and I would write that ranking as policy analysis for CIER. I expect to complete the Ph.D.\ in June 2027.

My job-market paper, ``Designing Return Policy in E-Commerce: A Trade-off between Purchase and Return Efficiency,'' treats return-shipping insurance as a marketplace rule on Taobao. A women's apparel retailer switched between automatic coverage, paid by the seller, and optional coverage, bought by the buyer. A stricter rule cuts wasteful returns, but the lower return rate makes a higher price more attractive and can shut out purchases that would have been worth making. I estimate purchase, insurance take-up, and returns under the seller's monopoly pricing by the generalized method of moments, across six apparel categories. Automatic coverage does better where margins and salvage value are high, because it brings in valuable purchases. Optional coverage does better where margins are thin, by about 15 to 30 percentage points of first-best welfare, and by about 50 percentage points in the thinnest category. A counterfactual menu, a discounted price plus a 10 to 15 percent restocking fee, beats both rules in most categories. That menu is a policy instrument: it trades participation against wasteful returns, and it changes the price the seller sets.

Two further projects ask the same style of question. With Ce Bian and Liang Zhao, I study pass-through when buyers can return the good. A platform commission or a sales tax can lower the equilibrium price and raise welfare. When a higher price makes a return more likely, the seller's effective margin shrinks and supply can slope down, so a higher fee can cut both the price and the return rate. An incidence calculation that treats the sale as final can get the sign of the policy wrong. With Kohei Kawaguchi and Kei Kawai, I study Japanese book retail under resale price maintenance. The retail price is fixed before demand is known, which supports inventory when demand is low, and unsold copies are returned and discarded. Ignoring inventory and uncertain demand overstates marginal cost by about 20 percent and misstates the environmental cost. We ask how much waste the commitment creates, and whether a deposit-refund tax can reduce it.

My M.Phil.\ thesis with Pak Hung Au shows that heterogeneous search costs sort buyers across products and can raise prices when search becomes cheaper, the margin a platform moves when it designs search. Earlier, as a research assistant at National Taiwan University, I modeled a bank crisis on an interbank lending network and asked when a regulator's no-bailout threat is credible.

At CIER I would turn these rankings into written policy analysis for digital markets.

\textbf{Platform return, refund, and fee rules.} I would move from one seller's insurance menu to rules a marketplace can set across sellers: who is covered, who pays the cost of a return, and how a restocking fee changes who participates and how much is returned. The welfare object is the purchase-versus-return trade-off in the job-market paper, now stated as platform policy.

\textbf{Platform fees and taxes when a purchase can be reversed.} Building on the work with Bian and Zhao, I would rank commission schedules and sales taxes when returns feed back into price, and state when a standard tax analysis misstates who pays.

\textbf{Pricing commitments and waste.} Building on the work with Kawaguchi and Kawai, I would compare the inventory benefit of a price that cannot adjust with the unsold stock and environmental cost it creates, including under rules that fix prices or limit discounting. For a merger or a conduct question in a digital market, I would use Ph.D.\ coursework in merger simulation, entry and exit, and conduct identification, and I would label that exercise as coursework until it is estimated on the market at issue.

CIER's specialty is economic policy analysis. I would write each ranking as a policy note: the marketplace rule, who buys and who returns, how the price moves, and what evidence would reverse the conclusion.
