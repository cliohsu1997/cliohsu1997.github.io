## Opening

Today we're going to talk about inflation. Simply put, it measures how fast prices go up over time. And we'll answer a few big questions: What is inflation? How do we measure it? Is it good or bad for society? And what can the central bank do about it — and at what cost?

Usually people think inflation is bad because the same money buys less. Groceries get more expensive, the bus ticket costs more, and if you still have a student loan, that burden feels heavier as living costs rise. That's why inflation feels unfair: you didn't do anything differently, yet your money slowly loses value.

## The receipt

The easiest place to start is something you already know how to read. Look at the slide. On the left are two real receipts from the same supermarket in Hadano: May 12, 2002 at JUSCO, and October 12, 2026 at AEON — same store family, since JUSCO later became AEON, same five items, same quantities.

Look at the table. Five-kilogram rice went from 980 yen to 4,200 yen — up 329 percent. One-liter milk went from 98 yen to 260 yen — up about 165 percent. The other groceries rose around 300 percent, and the whole basic basket roughly tripled, from 1,200 yen to about 5,200 yen.

If we average those five percentage increases and divide by 5, we get about a 259 percent rise over 24 years. That's the average across products, not year by year. For average inflation per year, take final expense over initial expense, take the 24th root, and subtract 1 — roughly 6 percent per year on average. Okay?

## Is 6% the inflation rate?

So — do you think 6 percent is the official inflation rate? Here we only looked at one shop, two days, five items. That's not the official number yet.

Because not everyone buys the same things, and not everyone feels the same rise. Look back at the table: milk rose 165 percent, while rice rose about twice as much. If your basket is mostly milk, your price rise is smaller than if your basket is mostly rice, eggs, and cabbage. Different shopping lists mean different inflation stories.

Without a shared basket, everyone has their own story. So we need a basket that stands in for consumers in general — and that's what the Consumer Price Index, or CPI, tracks over time.

## The Consumer Price Index

CPI is the cost of a fixed basket of goods and services compared to a base year. Right now the base year is 2025, so CPI in 2025 is set to 100. In 2026, CPI asks: how much did that same fixed basket go up? In Japan, the Statistics Bureau runs household surveys — asking families what they actually buy — and builds a basket from real consumer behavior. It's not your cart or my cart; it's the average consumer's cart.

In Japan, food is about 28 percent, housing 22, transport 14, health 5, and the rest — about 32 percent — covers education, clothing, culture, and recreation like going to the movies. Bigger spending shares get more weight. Milk and rice both matter, but rice matters more if households actually spend more on it.

Now look at the formula on the slide. Each item j has a fixed quantity q-j from the survey. Imagine two shopping trips with the exact same cart — only prices change. On the bottom is what that cart cost in the base year. On top is what that same cart costs today. Divide today's cost by the base-year cost, times 100 — that's the CPI. If CPI is 103, the basket costs 3 percent more than in the base year.

## Inflation, Deflation, and Disinflation

So far we've talked about a level — the CPI. But policymakers and newspapers usually talk about a rate. The inflation rate tells you how fast the CPI is changing and in which direction: this year's CPI minus last year's CPI, divided by last year's CPI — the slope of the CPI curve on the graph.

Inflation means CPI is rising, so the rate is positive. Deflation means CPI is falling — the rate is negative and the price level itself is dropping. At first that sounds nice because things get cheaper. On the right is disinflation: CPI is still rising, so inflation is positive, just at a smaller and smaller rate. Prices are still going up, just more slowly — that's disinflation, not deflation.

Why is deflation dangerous? When people expect cheaper prices next year, they delay spending. With less consumption, firms earn less and stop hiring. As incomes fall, consumers spend even less — a vicious cycle Japan lived through for years until recently.

## Example: milk and rice

Let's make this concrete with a quick example. Suppose the basket has two bags of rice and two liters of milk. We want to find CPI at T0, T1, and T2, compute the inflation rates, and check whether deflation happens at T2.

Here is the price table. Milk: 100 yen at the base, then 120, then 125. Rice: 200 at the base, then 240, then 250. Take a few seconds and work it out. [pause — then advance for the solution]

Solution. Basket cost equals two times the milk price plus two times the rice price. At T0: two times 100 plus two times 200 — 600 yen. We set CPI to 100. At T1: two times 120 plus two times 240 — 720 yen, so CPI is 100 times 720 over 600, which equals 120. At T2: two times 125 plus two times 250 — 750 yen, so CPI is 100 times 750 over 600, or 125.

Now the inflation rates. At T1: 120 minus 100, over 100 — 20 percent. At T2: 125 minus 120, over 120 — about 4.2 percent. CPI rose from 100 to 120 to 125, so both periods are inflation. The rate fell from 20 percent to 4.2 percent — that's disinflation, not deflation.

## Three costs of inflation

We've measured inflation. Before asking what causes it: is inflation only bad? Economists name three specific costs.

First, shoe-leather costs — the hassle of holding cash. When prices keep rising, cash in your wallet loses value. People hold less cash and make more trips to the bank or ATM, wasting time just protecting their money — like wearing out your shoes walking back and forth to the bank. Estimates suggest 10 percent inflation costs about 1 to 2 percent of GDP, and even 3 percent inflation costs roughly half a percent of GDP — around 40 billion dollars.

Second, from the firm's side, menu costs — the cost of changing prices. Businesses have to reprint menus, update websites, re-price shelves, and notify customers. And when some stores update prices fast while others lag, price confusion makes it harder to tell what things are really worth.

Third, and biggest: unexpected inflation. Think about loans and pensions — a pension is the regular retirement income you get every month after you stop working. Both usually pay a fixed amount in yen or dollars. When inflation jumps higher than expected, that fixed money buys less — quietly shifting wealth from one side to the other.

Take a car loan. Suppose the bank charges 5 percent because it expects 2 percent inflation, aiming for a 3 percent real return. If inflation jumps to 6 percent, you pay the bank back in money worth less. You, the borrower, come out ahead, and the bank loses about 1 percent after inflation. The same thing hurts retirees on a fixed pension: prices go up, their check stays the same, and they can buy less.

Even worse is uncertainty. When nobody can predict inflation, borrowers, workers, retirees, and businesses are all guessing. Most people hate that risk. It's like playing a game where the rules keep changing — nobody can plan, and that's costly for everyone.

## Benefits of inflation

Inflation isn't only costs — if it were, central banks would aim for zero. There are three benefits on the slide.

First, a little inflation encourages investment. When inflation is low and positive, cash in a bank account slowly loses value each year, pushing people to put money into productive assets like new business equipment instead of letting it sit idle.

Second, the real value of government debt gets diluted over time. Most public debt is fixed in yen or dollars, so gradual inflation slowly shrinks its real burden without rewriting contracts.

Third, a little inflation helps the job market. Companies rarely cut nominal pay — workers hate pay cuts. With 2 or 3 percent inflation, real wages can adjust smoothly when labor demand drops without anyone taking a pay cut. A 2 percent cut with zero inflation feels terrible; a 3 percent raise with 5 percent inflation still feels like a raise, even though buying power fell. That's why modest inflation can "grease the wheels" of the job market.

We'll skip seigniorage today — revenue from creating money — though it often appears in textbook lists.

## Hyperinflation

So far we've talked about ordinary inflation — a few percent a year. The extreme case is hyperinflation, when prices rise so fast they can double in hours or days.

The clearest modern example is Zimbabwe in 2008. Inflation exploded so fast that cash lost value almost in real time. Savings in local currency were wiped out, and people abandoned the Zimbabwean dollar for U.S. dollars and barter. Why? The government printed money to cover deficits. Once people lost trust, they spent cash as fast as they could, driving prices up even faster.

And this isn't just history. As of 2026 accounting updates, Argentina and Venezuela are still hyperinflationary economies, and Zimbabwe's new currency is still watched closely. That's why central banks care so much about keeping inflation under control.

## Tools against inflation

We've seen that inflation has costs — and in the extreme, hyperinflation. So what can policy do? Which tool a central bank reaches for depends on how costly inflation is, and as we'll see next, on what's causing it. Three tools.

### Tool 1: Interest rates

The tool you hear about most is the interest rate — the price of borrowing. If the central bank raises rates, loans get more expensive, so households and firms borrow, spend, and invest less. Demand cools and inflation eases. If it lowers rates, borrowing gets cheaper and spending picks up. The tradeoff: fighting inflation with higher rates often means slower growth.

### Tool 2: Quantitative easing & yield curve control — Japan's case

A second tool targets long-term interest rates when short-term rates are already stuck near zero. Long-term rates matter for mortgages, business investment, and government borrowing. To bring them down, a central bank buys long-term government bonds — **quantitative easing**, or QE — pumping cash into the financial system and pushing long rates down. **Yield curve control**, or YCC, uses the exact same bond-buying tool, just with a rate target instead of a quantity target: the bank buys whatever bonds are needed to pin long-term yields near a set level. Japan's Bank of Japan is the famous case — using QE in 2001, bigger asset buying in 2013, and YCC in 2016 to keep borrowing cheap across the curve even when short rates had no room left.

### Tool 3: Reserve requirements

A third tool works through quantity — how much money banks can create. Banks don't lend out every yen or dollar they take in; regulators require them to hold a share of deposits as idle reserves. Raise that reserve ratio, and banks lend less, credit tightens, and inflation pressure falls. Lower it, and banks can lend more. The idea is simple: control credit right at the source.

Okay — we know the toolkit. Next: before you pick a tool, you need to know what's driving inflation.

## Diagnosing the cause

Three channels — demand, supply, and expectations. I'll use the United States as examples.

First, demand. During COVID, factory shutdowns squeezed supply. Then governments tried to boost the economy by sending cash straight to households — what economists call "helicopter money," from Milton Friedman's metaphor of a helicopter dropping cash from the sky. Money landed straight in people's accounts, not through banks or interest rates. Spending jumped faster than firms could supply, and prices shot up.

Second, supply. Sometimes costs rise first. An oil shock — from a war in the Persian Gulf or a threat to the Strait of Hormuz — makes energy scarce and expensive, feeding into transport, plastics, heating, almost everything. A trade war works the same way: tariffs and shipping disruptions make imports and production more expensive, pushing prices up even without a spending boom.

Third, expectations. Suppose inflation starts out high because of a supply shock — say, a war pushes up oil prices. Even after the war ends and oil prices calm down, people expect prices to keep rising. So workers ask for higher wages to keep up, and companies raise prices to cover those higher wages. Higher prices lead to higher wages, which lead to higher prices — turning a temporary shock into inflation that sticks around.

## The output--inflation tradeoff

Different causes need different responses — even with the same toolkit. Here's why the cause matters when you adjust interest rates.

If inflation is mainly from hot demand — like helicopter money — cooling demand works. Raise rates, borrowing gets more expensive, spending slows, and inflation falls. The cost is slower growth: the classic output–inflation tradeoff.

But if inflation comes from a supply shock — oil, Hormuz, or a trade war — the picture flips. Rising costs push prices up while firms are already cutting production because inputs are scarce or expensive. You get prices up and output down at the same time: **stagflation** — stagnation plus inflation. If policymakers fight that only by cutting demand hard, they deepen the slump while energy costs stay high. So the lesson isn't "never cool demand" — it's: figure out the cause first.

## Case study: Japan through the CPI chart

Now let's look at Japan — the economy you live in, and one of the biggest inflation stories in modern history. This World Bank chart shows Japan's yearly CPI inflation. Above zero means prices are going up; below zero means deflation — prices are falling. Let's walk through it in six short chapters.

Chapter one: the bubble peak around 1990. Back in 1985, under the Plaza Accord, the U.S. pushed Japan to let the yen get stronger so American goods could compete better. The stronger yen hurt Japanese exports, so the Bank of Japan cut rates way down to protect the economy. Cheap money fueled a massive boom in stocks and real estate — at the peak, Tokyo's Imperial Palace grounds were estimated to equal all the land in California, and Japanese land overall was valued at five times the entire U.S. When the BOJ raised rates in 1989, stocks crashed in 1990 and land prices fell soon after. Look at the chart: CPI inflation was still around 3 percent in 1990–91, but firms and banks had borrowed heavily against overpriced land and were suddenly in deep trouble.

Chapter two: the long flat stretch around zero. After the bubble burst, spending dried up, banks were stuck with unpaid loans, and people waited to buy because they expected flat or falling prices. Inflation dropped below zero in 1995 and hugged zero for years — Japan's deflation era. The BOJ tried almost everything: zero rates in 1999, buying government bonds — QE — in 2001, bigger bond buying in 2013, negative rates in 2016, and pinning long-term rates — YCC — that same year. Remember Tool 2? That came straight from this period.

Chapter three: the 2008–09 dip. You see a quick jump in 2008, then a drop to about minus 1.4 percent in 2009 as oil prices crashed during the global financial crisis. Global commodity swings move Japan's line too.

Chapter four: the 2013–14 bump — Abenomics and the sales tax hike. In April 2013, the BOJ rolled out huge stimulus with a 2 percent inflation goal, and 2014 jumped to about 2.7 percent. Looks like it worked — but not so fast. Both the Statistics Bureau and the BOJ note that much of that jump came from raising the sales tax from 5 to 8 percent in April 2014. Take out the tax hike, and prices were still rising well below 2 percent.

Chapter five: the COVID dip. In 2020 inflation sat near zero, and in 2021 overall CPI slipped to minus 0.2 percent as pandemic lockdowns slowed spending.

Chapter six: the 2022 turnaround. Since April 2022, core inflation — CPI without fresh food — has stayed above 2 percent, with overall inflation hitting 4.3 percent in early 2023 and averaging 3.3 percent that year. First a weak yen made imported energy and food more expensive; then the 2024 spring wage talks brought the biggest pay raises in decades. In March 2024, the BOJ ended negative rates and YCC, lifting its short-term rate to 0 to 0.1 percent. After decades of fighting falling prices, Japan now has to deal with inflation that's actually back.

So don't just say "Japan had deflation." Read the whole chart step by step: bubble, long flat line, 2008 dip, tax-hike bump, COVID dip, and turnaround. Every move has a simple story behind it — and that's how you make sense of inflation in the real world.
