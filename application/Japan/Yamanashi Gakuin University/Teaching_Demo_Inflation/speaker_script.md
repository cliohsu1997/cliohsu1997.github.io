## Opening

Today we are going to talk about inflation. Simply put, inflation is how fast prices rise in the economy over time.

First: people usually think inflation is bad, because their money buys less. The same cash just doesn't get you as far. You see it everywhere — groceries climbing year after year, a bus ticket that costs more than last semester, rent eating a bigger chunk of the paycheck. When prices for almost everything keep rising, your paycheck just doesn't go as far. That's why inflation feels unfair: you didn't do anything differently, but your money lost value.

Second: inflation can also have benefits. A little inflation can push people to invest instead of letting cash sit idle, and it can help the job market adjust without painful pay cuts. So it's not just “inflation hurts.”

Third: if inflation is really costly, what can the central bank do to bring it down? We'll come back to that — what tools they have, and what those tools cost.

Let's start with something you've all seen: a receipt.

## The receipt

If we want to talk about rising prices, the easiest place to start is something you already know how to read. Look at the slide. Two real receipts, same supermarket in Hadano. May 12, 2002: JUSCO. October 12, 2026: AEON. Same store family — JUSCO later became AEON. Same five items. Same quantities.

Now the table. Rice, five kilos: 980 yen to 4,200 — up 329 percent. Milk, one litre: 98 to 260 — up 165 percent. Ten eggs: 78 to 310 — up 297 percent. A loaf of bread: 68 to 210 — up 209 percent. One cabbage: 58 to 230 — up 297 percent. The whole basket: 1,282 yen to 5,210 — up 306 percent.

Big increases, but over a long stretch. Average the five increases and divide by five: 259 percent — that's across products, not per year. For average inflation per year: final over initial, to the power of one over the years, minus one. So 5,210 over 1,282, to the power of one over 24, minus one — about 6 percent a year. Not one sudden jump. Roughly 6 percent a year, on average, over 24 years.

## Is 6% the inflation rate?

So — is 6 percent the inflation rate? One shop. Two days. Five items. That's a story from a receipt, not the official number.

Here's another problem. People don't buy the same things, so they didn't feel the same rise. Look back. Milk rose 165 percent. Rice rose 329 percent. Mostly buy milk? Prices rose a lot less for you than if you mostly buy rice. Bread sits in between. Same store, same years, different shopping lists — so different people feel inflation differently.

Even with just five items, people feel different price rises. How much should each item count? Whose list should we use — the milk drinker's, or the rice buyer's? That's exactly what the official rate has to answer. And the answer is the Consumer Price Index.

## The Consumer Price Index

That was the problem: without a shared basket and shared weights, everyone has their own inflation story. The CPI is how we get from “whose milk, whose rice?” to one official number. Three things.

First, the definition. The CPI is the cost of a fixed basket of goods and services, compared to a base year. Same basket, different dates. Not your list, not mine — one shared basket for the country. How is it chosen? From what households actually buy. In Japan, the Statistics Bureau runs household spending surveys, asks many households what they purchase, then builds the basket and its weights from average spending in a base year — right now, 2025. So it's an average shopping list for the country, not any one person's.

Second, the weights. Once you have a basket, you still need to say how much each item counts. A weight is just category spending divided by total spending. In Japan: food about 28 percent, housing about 22, transport about 14, health about 5. The rest — about 32 percent — covers education, clothing, fuel and utilities, furniture, recreation, and so on. So the Bureau doesn't pick the milk drinker or the rice buyer. It uses average spending shares. Bigger share of spending, bigger weight. Milk and rice both matter — but only by how much households actually spend on them.

Third, the formula. Now put it in one calculation. Look at the box. Each item j has a basket quantity, q-j — fixed from the survey. Think of two trips with the exact same cart: same items, same quantities. The cart doesn't change. Only prices do. Bottom of the fraction: what did that cart cost in the base year? Base-year prices times those fixed quantities. Top: what does the same cart cost today? Today's prices times the same quantities. Divide today by the base year, times one hundred — that's the CPI. CPI of 103 means the basket costs 3 percent more than in the base year.

## Inflation, Deflation, and Disinflation

So far we have a level — the CPI. But policymakers and newspapers talk about a rate. The CPI is a level: how expensive the basket is now. The inflation rate is how fast that level is changing, and in which direction. Formula: this year's CPI minus last year's CPI, divided by last year's CPI. That percent change is the slope of the CPI curve.

Inflation: CPI rising, rate positive, prices higher than before. Deflation: CPI falling, rate negative — the price level itself is dropping. Disinflation: CPI still rising, but more slowly — still positive, just smaller than before. The slope is flattening.

Look at the graph. Time runs left to right. Vertical axis: the CPI. On the left: inflation — CPI rising, curve up steeply. In the middle: deflation — CPI falling, curve turns down. Falling prices sound good. They're not. If people expect cheaper prices next year, they wait to spend. Firms earn less, stop hiring. Incomes fall, and a recession gets more likely. Japan lived with this for years: low inflation and, at times, falling prices — including all-items CPI of minus 0.2 percent in 2021. That's why the Bank of Japan aims for 2 percent, not zero. On the right: disinflation — CPI still rising, so inflation is still positive, but each step up is smaller. Prices still rising, just more slowly. Not deflation. Deflation would need the curve itself to fall.

## Example: milk and rice

We've defined CPI and the inflation rate. Let's make it concrete so you can compute it yourself. Basket: two litres of milk, two bags of rice. Quantities stay fixed. Only prices change.

Here's the question. Compute the CPI at t-zero, t-one, and t-two. Then the inflation rate at t-one and t-two. At t-two the CPI rose again — is that deflation?

Prices. Milk: 100 yen at the base, then 120, then 125. Rice: 200, then 240, then 250. Take a few seconds and work it out. [pause — then advance for the solution]

Solution. Basket cost: two times the milk price plus two times the rice price. Base, t-zero: two times 100 plus two times 200 — 200 plus 400 — 600 yen. Set CPI to 100. At t-one: two times 120 plus two times 240 — 240 plus 480 — 720. CPI is one hundred times 720 over 600 — 120. At t-two: two times 125 plus two times 250 — 250 plus 500 — 750. CPI is one hundred times 750 over 600 — 125.

Now the rates. At t-one: 120 minus 100, over 100 — 20 percent. At t-two: 125 minus 120, over 120 — about 4.2 percent. CPI went 100, 120, 125 — so both periods are inflation; the price level is still going up. But the rate fell from 20 to about 4.2 percent. Prices still rising, just more slowly. That's disinflation — not deflation. Deflation would need the CPI itself to fall.

## Three costs of inflation

We've measured inflation. Before we ask what causes it: is inflation only bad? Remember the opening — people hate inflation because their paycheck doesn't go as far. Economists go further and name specific costs. Three to start.

Cost one: shoe-leather — the hassle of holding cash. When prices keep rising, cash in your wallet loses value. So people hold less cash and make more trips to the bank or ATM. Time spent managing money instead of doing something useful. Economists call it the shoe-leather cost — like wearing out your shoes going back and forth to the bank. Even moderate inflation makes people waste time just protecting the value of their money.

Cost two: menu costs — the cost of changing prices. Rising prices are not free to update. When inflation is high, businesses change prices more often: new menus, updated websites, re-priced shelves, telling customers. Those are menu costs. There's a second problem too: price confusion. If some businesses re-price fast and others lag, it gets harder to tell what things are really worth. Buying, selling, and planning all get harder.

Cost three: unexpected inflation moves wealth around — and creates uncertainty. The most important cost, and the one that catches people off guard. Loans and pensions are written in fixed yen or dollars. When inflation turns out higher or lower than people expected, it quietly moves money from one group to another.

Here's how it works with loans. You borrow 10,000 dollars to buy a car. The bank charges 5 percent because it expects inflation of 2 — so it expects to earn about 3 percent after inflation. Fair deal. But if inflation actually turns out to be 6, the bank earns about minus 1 after inflation. You win, because you pay back in dollars worth less. The bank loses. Flip it — lower-than-expected inflation — and the opposite happens.

A real example. In 1960, a typical mortgage ran about 6 percent. People expected inflation around 2.5, so the bank expected about 3.5 after inflation. Actual inflation over the life of that mortgage averaged 5. So the bank earned only about 1 after inflation. Great deal for the borrower — at the bank's expense.

Same thing with pensions. Retired on a fixed pension? Higher-than-expected inflation hurts you. The pension buys less, and you can't do anything about it. You're like the bank in the mortgage example — paid back in dollars worth less.

The bigger problem is uncertainty. When inflation is unpredictable, nobody knows what to expect. Borrowers and lenders guess. Workers and retirees guess. Businesses guess. And most people dislike guessing about money. So unpredictable inflation makes everyone worse off, even the accidental winners. It's like a game where the rules keep changing — you might win, but you can't plan, and that is stressful and costly.

## Benefits of inflation

So inflation is not only costs. If it were only bad, central banks would aim for zero. There are benefits. Two are on the slide.

First: a little inflation can encourage investment. Start from cash. With low but positive inflation, cash in a checking account loses a little value each year. That nudges people to put money into things that grow — stocks, equipment, new businesses. Some economists say a little inflation pushes money into real investment instead of letting it sit still. Not an argument for high inflation — but one reason some central banks aim for a small positive rate instead of zero.

Second: a little inflation helps the job market. Here's the sticky part. Companies rarely cut pay. Workers hate pay cuts. But sometimes what wages are really worth needs to fall — say, when demand for a certain type of worker drops. With zero inflation, the only way to cut real buying power is an actual pay cut — hard to do. With a little inflation — say 2 or 3 percent — real buying power can fall gradually without anyone taking a pay cut. A 2 percent cut with no inflation feels terrible. A 3 percent raise with 5 percent inflation feels like a raise — even though buying power went down. So a little inflation can grease the wheels of the job market and help keep unemployment lower.

We're skipping seigniorage today — the money the government gains by creating new currency — even though it shows up in textbook lists of benefits.

## Hyperinflation

So far, ordinary inflation — a few percent a year, or the higher rates after COVID. There is a much more extreme case. When prices rise so fast they can double in hours or days, economists call it hyperinflation.

The clearest modern example: Zimbabwe. In 2008, inflation exploded. At the peak, prices were rising so fast people watched the value of cash collapse almost in real time. Savings in the local currency were wiped out. People stopped trusting the Zimbabwean dollar. They turned to foreign currency — mainly the U.S. dollar — and to barter. The local money stopped doing its job.

Why so bad? The government printed money to cover budget gaps. Once people lost confidence, they spent cash as fast as they could, before it lost more value — and that pushed prices up even faster.

And this is not only history. As of recent 2026 accounting updates, Argentina and Venezuela are still treated as hyperinflationary economies. Zimbabwe introduced a new currency after abandoning the old dollar, but it's still watched closely — losing trust in money is not fixed overnight. That's why central banks care so much about keeping inflation under control.

## Tools against inflation

We've seen inflation has costs — and in the extreme, hyperinflation. So what can policy do? Key idea: which tool you reach for depends on how costly inflation is — and, as we'll see, on the cause. Three tools.

### Tool 1: Interest rates

The tool you hear about most: the interest rate. Think of it as the price of borrowing. The central bank raises rates — loans get more expensive. Households and firms borrow less, spend less, invest less. Spending cools, and that can bring inflation down. Lower rates — borrowing gets cheaper, spending tends to rise, inflation pressure can go up. The tradeoff: fighting inflation with higher rates often means slower growth. That's the cost of using this tool hard.

### Tool 2: Yield curve control — Japan's case

A second tool: long-term borrowing costs, not just short-term rates. Short rates are not the whole story. Long rates matter for mortgages, business investment, and government borrowing. Yield curve control means the central bank aims at those long rates too. The famous case: Japan's central bank. When short rates were already near zero, it bought government bonds as needed to keep long-term rates near a target — long-term borrowing stayed cheap. So even when the usual short-rate tool had little room left, Japan tried to keep stimulus flowing through long rates.

### Tool 3: Reserve requirements

A third tool works through quantity — how much money banks can create. Banks don't lend out every yen or dollar they take in. Regulators can require them to keep a share of deposits as reserves — cash sitting idle. Raise that reserve ratio: banks must hold more, can lend less. Lending tightens, money creation slows, spending cools, inflation pressure falls. Lower it: banks can lend more, credit expands, spending can rise. The idea is simple: control lending at the source — how much banks are allowed to create.

That's the toolkit. Next: before you choose a tool, you need to know what's driving inflation.

## Diagnosing the cause

Three channels — demand, supply, expectations. I'll use the United States for examples.

First, demand. During COVID, a lot of manufacturing shut down. Factories closed. Supply was disrupted. Then the government tried to boost the economy by piping money in — stimulus. One form: cash straight to households — “helicopter money.” Money landed in people's accounts. Spending jumped faster than firms could supply. Too much spending chasing goods — prices get pulled up. So it was a combination: supply already tight from shutdowns, then a big demand push from stimulus.

Second, supply. Demand isn't the only path. Sometimes costs rise first — the cost side. An oil shock — a war in the Persian Gulf, a threat to the Strait of Hormuz — makes energy scarce and expensive. Oil feeds into transport, plastics, heating, almost everything. A trade war fits here too: tariffs up, shipping disrupted, imports and production more expensive. Same idea — supply tight or costlier — prices pushed up even without a spending boom.

Third, expectations. Even after a shock fades, people form beliefs about what comes next. Suppose inflation is already high. Workers expect prices to keep rising, so they ask for higher wages. Firms pass those labor costs into prices. Inflation stays high, expectations rise again, and the loop continues. That wage–price spiral can turn a short-term shock into inflation that sticks around.

## The output--inflation tradeoff

Different causes need different responses — even with the same toolkit. We already saw interest rates. Here's why the cause matters when you use them.

If inflation is mainly hot demand — helicopter money — cooling demand can be the right move. Raise rates. Borrowing gets more expensive. Spending and investment cool. Inflation comes down. The cost: slower growth. That's the classic output–inflation tradeoff: lower inflation, weaker economy.

But if inflation comes from a supply shock — oil, Hormuz, trade war — the picture is different. Costs are already pushing prices up, and firms are already cutting production because inputs are scarce or expensive. So you can get prices up and output down at the same time. That's stagflation: stagnation plus inflation — weak economy, rising prices, together.

If policymakers then fight that inflation by cutting demand hard — as if it were the COVID stimulus case — they deepen the slump while energy costs are still high. So the lesson isn't “never cool demand.” It's: figure out the cause first. Demand-driven and supply-driven inflation are not the same problem — and treating a Hormuz-style oil shock like plain hot demand is how you walk into stagflation.

## Case study: Japan through the CPI chart

Now Japan — the economy you live in, and one of the longest inflation experiments in modern history. This line chart is Japan's annual CPI inflation rate, from the World Bank series on FRED. Above zero: prices rising. Below zero: deflation — the price level itself falling. Six chapters. About three to five minutes. Background first, then the claim.

Chapter one: the bubble peak around 1990. Background first. In 1985, under the Plaza Accord, the U.S. pushed Japan to let the yen get stronger — to shrink America's large trade gap. The yen surged. Japanese exports fell. Recession risk rose. So the BOJ cut rates hard to cushion the economy. Cheap credit lasted for years and fueled land and stock speculation. How extreme? At the peak, the Imperial Palace grounds — about three-quarters of a square mile — were estimated worth about the same as all the land in California. Japanese land overall: about five times U.S. land. And Tokyo real estate was said to top the entire United States. Then the BOJ hiked from 1989 over inflation and overheating worries. Stocks peaked at end-1989 and crashed in 1990. Land prices peaked later and kept falling. On the chart, CPI inflation is still around 3 percent in 1990–91 — consumer prices hadn't fallen yet — but the asset boom was already breaking. Firms and banks that had borrowed against high land values suddenly looked much weaker. That debt-and-collateral damage is the background for everything that follows.

Chapter two: the long near-zero and negative stretch. After the bubble, demand stayed weak. Bad loans piled up. People expected flat or falling prices, so they waited to spend. On the chart, CPI inflation turns negative in 1995 and hugs zero from below for years — the deflation era. The BOJ kept expanding its toolkit: zero rates in 1999, quantitative easing in 2001 — printing money to buy assets — a bigger version in 2013, a negative policy rate in 2016, and yield curve control the same year. Remember Tool 2? That's this chapter.

Chapter three: the 2008–09 dip. Brief bump in 2008, then a sharp drop — about minus 1.4 percent in 2009. Why? Oil and energy had surged, then crashed after the global financial crisis, and demand weakened. So the chart also moves with global commodity swings — not just Japan-only stories.

Chapter four: the 2013–14 uptick — Abenomics and the tax hike. April 2013: big monetary easing and a 2 percent price target. On the chart, 2014 jumps to about 2.7 or 2.8 percent. But careful. The Statistics Bureau and the BOJ both note that much of that jump was the April 2014 consumption-tax hike, from 5 to 8 percent. Strip out the tax, and inflation without that bump stayed well below 2 percent. The line moves up — but partly from a tax change, not only new inflation psychology.

Chapter five: the COVID dip. 2020: about zero. 2021: about minus 0.2 percent on the all-items CPI. The pandemic lull pulled prices down again — just when Japan had spent years trying to push them up.

Chapter six: the 2022-and-after pickup. From April 2022, CPI without fresh food stayed above the BOJ's 2 percent target. Overall inflation peaked near 4.3 percent in early 2023 and averaged around 3.3 percent that year. Drivers: first a weaker yen and higher imported energy and goods costs; then stronger wages — the 2024 spring talks delivered the largest pay hikes in decades. March 2024: the BOJ ended negative rates and yield curve control, and set the short-rate target at 0 to 0.1 percent. Same chart, new chapter: Japan spent decades fighting deflation. Now the question is how to manage inflation that has finally returned.

So don't just say “Japan had deflation.” Read the periods: bubble, long flat line, crisis dip, tax-and-easing bump, COVID dip, then the recent turnaround. Each move has a background — that's how you diagnose inflation in the real world.
