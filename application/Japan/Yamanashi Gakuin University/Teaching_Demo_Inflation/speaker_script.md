## Opening

Today we're going to talk about inflation. Simply put, it measures how fast prices go up over time. And we're going to try to answer a few big questions.

What is inflation? How do we measure it? Is it good or bad for society? And what can the central bank do about it — and at what cost?

Usually people think inflation is bad because the same money buys less. Groceries get more expensive, the bus ticket costs more, and if you still have a student loan, that burden feels heavier as living costs rise. That's why inflation feels unfair. You didn't do anything differently — but your money slowly loses value.

## The receipt

If we want to talk about rising prices, the easiest place to start is something you already know how to read. Look at the slide. On the left are two real receipts from the same supermarket in Hadano. May 12, 2002: JUSCO. October 12, 2026: AEON. Same store family — JUSCO later became AEON. Same five items. Same quantities.

Now look at the table. Five-kilogram rice: from 980 yen to 4,200 yen — up 329 percent. Milk, one liter: from 98 yen to 260 yen — up about 165 percent. The other groceries rise around 300 percent over those years. The whole basic basket goes from 1,200 yen to about 5,200 yen — roughly threefold.

If we average those five percentage increases and divide by 5, we get about a 259 percent rise over 24 years. That's the average across products, not year by year. For average inflation per year, take final expense over initial expense, take the 24th root, and subtract 1. You get about 6 percent a year. So over 24 years, the basket rises by roughly 6 percent per year on average. Okay?

## Is 6% the inflation rate?

So — do you think 6% is the official inflation rate? Here we only looked at one shop, two days, five items. That's not the official number yet.

Because not everyone buys the same thing, and not everyone feels the same rise. Look back at the table: milk rose 165%, and rice rose much more — about twice that. So if your basket is mostly milk, your price rise is smaller than if your basket is mostly rice, eggs, and cabbage. Different shopping lists, different inflation stories.

The problem is, without a shared basket, everyone has their own inflation story. So we need a basket that stands in for consumers in general — and then we watch how the consumer price index, the CPI, tracks the price of that basket over time.

## The Consumer Price Index

So CPI is the cost of a fixed basket of goods and services compared to a base year. Right now the base year is 2025. So CPI in 2025 is set to 100. In 2026, CPI asks: how much did that same fixed basket go up? The Statistics Bureau picks the basket. In Japan, they run household surveys — they ask a lot of families what they actually buy — and then build a basket from how real consumers behave. It's not your cart or my cart. It's the average consumer's cart.

In Japan, food is about 28 percent, housing about 22, transport about 14, health about 5, and the rest — about 32 percent — covers education, clothing, culture, recreation, going to the movies, that kind of thing. The Bureau doesn't pick one special consumer with one weird shopping habit. It uses average spending shares. Bigger share of spending means more weight on that price change. Milk and rice both matter — but rice matters more if households actually spend more on it.

Now put it in one calculation. Look at the box on the slide. Each item j has a basket quantity, q-j — fixed from the survey. Imagine two shopping trips with the exact same cart: same items, same amounts. The cart doesn't change. Only prices do. Bottom: what did that cart cost in the base year? Base-year prices times those fixed quantities. Top: what does the same cart cost today? Today's prices times the same quantities. Divide today by the base year, times one hundred — that's the CPI. If the CPI is 103, the same basket costs 3 percent more than in the base year.

## Inflation, Deflation, and Disinflation

So far we've been talking about a level — the CPI. But policymakers and newspapers usually talk about a rate. The inflation rate tells you how fast the CPI is changing, and in which direction. The formula is simple. It is calculated as this year's CPI minus last year's CPI divided by last year's CPI. That change is the slope of the CPI curve as shown on the graph.

Inflation means the CPI is rising, so the rate of change is positive. Prices are higher than before. Deflation means the CPI is falling. The rate of change is negative, and the price level itself is decreasing. What makes deflation seem good is that goods become cheaper, so consumers can buy more goods. Finally on the right, we have disinflation. The CPI is still rising, so inflation is positive. But the rate of increase is smaller. It's getting smaller and smaller. Prices are rising just more slowly. That's not deflation. That's disinflation. Note that this is not deflation because deflation requires the price level itself to drop. However, when people expect cheaper prices next year, they defer their spending. With less consumption, firms earn less and stop hiring. When income falls, consumers spend even less. This creates a vicious cycle. Japan lived with this for years. Only until very recently, the CPI started to turn positive.

## Example: milk and rice

We've defined CPI and inflation rates. Let's make this concrete by walking through a tiny example. The consumer basket now consists of two bags of rice and two liters of milk. Quantity gives the weight of the basket, and we want to compute the CPI at T0, T1, and T2, and the corresponding inflation rate. And finally, we want to understand whether deflation happens at T2.

Here is the price table. Milk: 100 yen at the base, then 120, then 125. Rice: 200 at the base, then 240, then 250. Take a few seconds and work it out. [pause — then advance for the solution]

Solution. Basket cost equals two times the milk price plus two times the rice price. At the base, t-zero: two times 100 plus two times 200 — that is 200 plus 400 — 600 yen. We set CPI to 100. At t-one: two times 120 plus two times 240 — 240 plus 480 — 720. CPI equals one hundred times 720 over 600 — 120. At t-two: two times 125 plus two times 250 — 250 plus 500 — 750. CPI equals one hundred times 750 over 600 — 125.

Now the inflation rates. At t-one: 120 minus 100, over 100 — 20 percent. At t-two: 125 minus 120, over 120 — about 4.2 percent. The CPI rose from 100 to 120 to 125, so both periods are inflation — the price level is still going up. But the inflation rate fell from 20 percent to about 4.2 percent. Prices are still rising, just more slowly. That is disinflation — not deflation. Deflation would require the CPI itself to fall.

## Three costs of inflation

Okay — we've measured inflation. Before we ask what causes it: is inflation only bad? Remember the opening — people hate it because their paycheck doesn't stretch as far. Economists go a step further and name specific costs.

Start with shoe-leather costs — the hassle of holding cash. When prices keep rising, cash in your wallet loses value. So people hold less cash and make more trips to the bank or the ATM. They spend time managing money instead of doing something useful. Economists call that the shoe-leather cost — like wearing out your shoes going back and forth to the bank. Even moderate inflation makes people waste time and energy just protecting the value of their money.

How big are these costs in total? Estimates suggest 10% inflation costs about 1% to 2% of GDP, and even 3% inflation costs about half a percent of GDP — roughly 40 billion dollars at the time of the estimate. So these aren't just textbook labels. Even moderate inflation is expensive for the economy as a whole.

From the firm's side, raising prices isn't free either — that's menu costs, the cost of changing prices. When inflation is high, businesses have to change prices more often: new menus, updated websites, re-priced shelves, telling customers. Those are menu costs. There's also price confusion. If some businesses change prices fast and others lag, it's harder for everyone to tell what things are really worth. That makes buying, selling, and planning harder.

And here's the biggest cost: unexpected inflation. Think about loans and pensions — a pension is the regular retirement income you get every month after you stop working. Both usually pay a fixed amount in yen or dollars. So when inflation jumps higher than people expected, that fixed money buys less — it quietly shifts wealth from one side to the other.

Take a car loan. Suppose the bank charges you 5 percent because it expects 2 percent inflation — so it expects to earn 3 percent in real terms. But if inflation jumps to 6 percent, you pay the bank back in money that's worth less. You, the borrower, come out ahead, and the bank actually loses about 1 percent after inflation. The same thing hurts retirees on a fixed pension: prices go up, their check stays the same, and they can buy less.

Even worse is the uncertainty. When nobody can predict inflation, borrowers, workers, retirees, and businesses are all guessing. Most people hate that kind of risk. It's like playing a game where the rules keep changing — even if you get lucky once, nobody can plan, and that's bad for everyone.

## Benefits of inflation

So inflation isn't only costs. If it were only bad, central banks would aim for zero. There are benefits too. Three of them are on the slide.

First: a little inflation can encourage investment. When inflation is low but positive, cash in a bank account slowly loses value every year. That pushes people to pull money out and put it into the real economy — like new business equipment — instead of letting it sit there. That's one reason some central banks aim for a small positive rate instead of zero.

Second: the real value of government debt can get diluted over time. Most public debt is written in fixed yen or dollars. When prices rise gradually, each yen of debt buys less in real terms — so the burden on public finances eases a bit without rewriting the contracts. Again, that's modest inflation, not a runaway case.

Third: a little inflation helps the job market. Companies rarely cut workers' pay — people hate pay cuts. But sometimes real wages need to fall when demand for a certain kind of worker drops. With zero inflation, that means a nominal cut, and that's hard. With a little inflation — say 2 or 3 percent — real wages can ease down without anyone taking a pay cut. A 2 percent cut with no inflation feels terrible. A 3 percent raise with 5 percent inflation still feels like a raise, even though buying power fell. So a little inflation can “grease the wheels” of the job market and help keep unemployment lower.

We won't focus on seigniorage today — revenue from creating money — even though it sometimes shows up in textbook lists of benefits.

## Hyperinflation

So far we've talked about ordinary inflation — a few percent a year, or even the higher rates after COVID. There's a much more extreme case. When prices rise so fast they can double in hours or days, economists call that hyperinflation.

The clearest modern example is Zimbabwe. In 2008, inflation exploded. At the peak, prices were rising so fast that cash was losing value almost in real time. Savings in the local currency got wiped out. People stopped trusting the Zimbabwean dollar. They switched to foreign currency — mainly the U.S. dollar — and to barter. Local money stopped doing its job.

Why did it get that bad? The government printed money to cover deficits. Once people lost confidence, they tried to spend cash as fast as they could, before it lost value again — and that made prices rise even faster.

And this isn't only history. As of recent 2026 accounting updates, Argentina and Venezuela are still treated as hyperinflationary economies. Zimbabwe brought in a new currency after ditching the old dollar, but it's still watched closely — losing trust in money doesn't disappear overnight. That's why central banks care so much about keeping inflation under control.

## Tools against inflation

We've seen that inflation has costs — and in the extreme, hyperinflation. So what can policy do? The key idea is: which tool you reach for depends on how costly inflation is, and later we'll see it also depends on the cause. Three tools.

### Tool 1: Interest rates

The tool you hear about most is the interest rate. Think of it as the price of borrowing. If the central bank raises rates, loans get more expensive. Households and firms borrow less, spend less, invest less. Demand cools — and that can bring inflation down. If the central bank lowers rates, borrowing gets cheaper, spending tends to rise, and inflation pressure can go up. There's a tradeoff: fighting inflation with higher rates often means slower growth. That's the cost of using this tool hard.

### Tool 2: Quantitative easing & yield curve control — Japan's case

A second tool targets long-term interest rates when short-term rates are already stuck near zero. Long-term rates matter for mortgages, business investment, and government borrowing. To bring those down, a central bank buys long-term government bonds — that's **quantitative easing**, or QE. Under standard QE, the central bank buys a set amount of bonds each month to pump cash into the financial system and push long-term rates down. **Yield curve control**, or YCC, uses the exact same bond-buying tool, just with a rate target instead of a quantity target: the central bank buys whatever bonds are needed to pin long-term yields near a set level. Japan's Bank of Japan is the famous case — using QE in 2001, bigger asset buying in 2013, and YCC in 2016 to keep borrowing cheap across the curve even when short rates had no room left.

### Tool 3: Reserve requirements

A third tool works through quantity — how much money banks can create. Banks don't lend out every yen or dollar they take in. Regulators can make them keep a share of deposits as reserves — cash sitting idle. Raise that reserve ratio, and banks must hold more and can lend less. Credit tightens, money creation slows, demand cools, inflation pressure falls. Lower the ratio, and banks can lend more, credit expands, spending can rise. The idea is simple: control credit at the source — how much banks are allowed to create.

Okay — we know the toolkit. Next: before you pick a tool, you need to know what's driving inflation.

## Diagnosing the cause

Three channels — demand, supply, and expectations. I'll use the United States as examples.

First, demand. During COVID, a lot of manufacturing shut down. Factories closed. That hit supply. Then people and the government tried to stimulate the economy by piping money into it — fiscal stimulus. One form was sending cash straight to households — “helicopter money.” Why that name? Milton Friedman had a metaphor: imagine a helicopter dropping cash from the sky. The point is money goes straight to people — into their accounts — not mainly through banks or interest rates. When people receive money in their accounts, spending jumps faster than firms can supply. That upward pressure on prices can cause inflation. And that's exactly what happened in COVID: supply was already tight from the shutdowns, and then came a big demand push — caused by the fiscal stimulus.

Second, supply. Demand isn't the only path. Sometimes costs rise first. That's the cost side. An oil shock — a war in the Persian Gulf, or a threat to the Strait of Hormuz — makes energy scarce and expensive. Oil feeds into transport, plastics, heating, almost everything. A trade war fits here too: tariffs go up, shipping gets disrupted, imports and production get more expensive. Same idea — supply is tight or more costly — so prices get pushed up even without a demand boom.

Third, expectations. Suppose inflation starts out high because of a supply shock — say, a war pushes up oil prices. Even after the war ends and oil prices calm down, people expect prices to keep rising. So workers ask for higher wages to keep up, and companies raise their prices to cover those higher wages. Higher prices lead to higher wages, which lead to higher prices — and that loop turns a temporary shock into inflation that sticks around.

## The output--inflation tradeoff

So different causes need different responses — even with the same toolkit. We already saw interest rates as a tool. Here's why the cause matters when you use them.

If inflation is mainly from hot demand — helicopter money — cooling demand can be the right move. Raise rates. Borrowing gets more expensive. Spending and investment cool. Inflation comes down. The cost is slower growth. That's the classic output–inflation tradeoff: lower inflation, but weaker output.

But if inflation is from a supply shock — oil, Hormuz, trade war — the picture is different. Costs are already pushing prices up, and firms are already cutting production because inputs are scarce or expensive. So you can get prices up and output down at the same time. We call that stagflation: stagnation plus inflation.

If policymakers then fight that inflation only by cutting demand hard — as if it were the COVID stimulus case — they deepen the slump while energy costs are still high. So the lesson isn't “never cool demand.” It's: figure out the cause first. Demand-driven inflation and supply-driven inflation are not the same problem — and treating a Hormuz-style oil shock like it's only hot demand is how you walk into stagflation.

## Case study: Japan through the CPI chart

Now let's look at Japan — the economy you actually live in, and one of the biggest inflation stories in modern history. This chart shows Japan's yearly CPI inflation from the World Bank. Above zero means prices are going up. Below zero means deflation — prices are actually falling. Let's walk through it in six short chapters.

Chapter one: the bubble peak around 1990. Back in 1985, under a deal called the Plaza Accord, the U.S. pushed Japan to let the yen get stronger so American goods could compete better. The yen shot up, Japanese exporters got hurt, and people worried about a recession. To help the economy, the Bank of Japan cut interest rates way down. Money stayed super cheap for years, and people poured it into stocks and real estate. Just how crazy did prices get? At the peak, people estimated the Imperial Palace grounds in Tokyo — less than one square mile — were worth as much as all the land in California. All the land in Japan was valued at about five times all the land in the U.S., and Tokyo alone was said to be worth more than the entire United States. Eventually, the Bank of Japan worried things were getting out of hand, so it started raising rates in 1989. Stocks crashed in 1990, and land prices fell soon after. Look at the chart: in 1990 and '91, everyday prices were still rising around 3 percent a year. But the bubble had already popped. Companies and banks had borrowed huge amounts based on those high land prices, and suddenly they were in deep trouble.

Chapter two: the long flat stretch around zero. After the bubble burst, people and businesses stopped spending. Banks were stuck with loans that wouldn't get paid back. And since everyone expected prices to stay flat — or even drop — people waited to buy things. On the chart, inflation drops below zero in 1995 and basically sits near zero for years. That's Japan's deflation era. The Bank of Japan tried almost everything: zero interest rates in 1999, buying government bonds — QE — in 2001, even bigger bond buying in 2013, negative rates in 2016, and pinning long-term rates — YCC — that same year. Remember Tool 2 from earlier? That came straight from this period.

Chapter three: the 2008–09 dip. You see a quick jump in 2008, and then a sharp drop — down to about minus 1.4 percent in 2009. Why? Oil and energy prices spiked in 2008, then crashed when the global financial crisis hit and people around the world cut back spending. So this chart isn't only about Japan — big swings in global oil and food prices move this line too.

Chapter four: the 2013–14 bump — Abenomics and the sales tax hike. In April 2013, the Bank of Japan rolled out massive stimulus and set a 2 percent inflation goal. On the chart, 2014 jumps to about 2.7 or 2.8 percent. Looks like it worked, right? Wait — not so fast. Both the Statistics Bureau and the Bank of Japan point out that a big part of that jump came from raising the sales tax from 5 percent to 8 percent in April 2014. If you take out the tax hike, prices were still rising way below 2 percent. So the line jumped mostly because of a tax hike, not because people expected prices to keep climbing.

Chapter five: the COVID dip. In 2020, inflation sits right around zero. In 2021, overall CPI drops to about minus 0.2 percent. With people staying home during the pandemic, spending slowed down and pulled prices back down — right when Japan had spent years trying to push them up.

Chapter six: the 2022 turnaround. Starting in April 2022, core inflation — that's CPI without fresh food — stayed above the Bank of Japan's 2 percent goal. Overall inflation even hit around 4.3 percent in early 2023 and averaged about 3.3 percent for the year. Why? First, a weak yen made imported energy and food much more expensive. Then wages started going up too — the 2024 spring wage talks brought the biggest pay raises in decades. In March 2024, the Bank of Japan finally ended negative interest rates and yield curve control, raising its short-term rate to 0 to 0.1 percent. Same chart, totally new story: after decades of fighting falling prices, Japan now has to deal with inflation that's actually back.

So the big lesson isn't just “Japan had deflation.” Read the whole chart step by step: the bubble, the long flat line, the 2008 crash dip, the tax-hike bump, the COVID dip, and then the turnaround. Every move on the chart has a simple story behind it — and that's how you make sense of inflation in the real world.
