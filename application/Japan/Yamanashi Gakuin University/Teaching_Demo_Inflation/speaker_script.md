## Opening

Good morning. Today we are going to answer three questions. What is inflation, and how is it measured? Is inflation good or bad? And what do central banks do — and at what cost? Why should you care? Because this shows up in your life every week. Your groceries. Your rent. Your bus fare. Your wages — whether they keep up or not. Your savings, quietly buying less than they did last year. And when central banks act, that reaches you too: your mortgage, your student loan, your credit card, and whether it is easy to find a job. So today we will look at what the numbers actually measure, when rising prices hurt and when they are normal, and what the central bank can do — and what that costs. Let's start with something you have all seen: a receipt.

## The receipt

On the left are two real receipts from the same supermarket in Hadano. The older one is from JUSCO, on 12 May 2002. The newer one is from AEON, on 12 October 2026. JUSCO was the store's name then. The chain was later renamed AEON. Same store family. Same five items. Same quantities. Now look at the table. Rice, five kilograms: from 980 yen to 4,200 yen — up 329 percent. Milk, one litre: from 98 to 260 — up 165 percent. Ten eggs: from 78 to 310 — up 297 percent. One loaf of bread: from 68 to 210 — up 209 percent. One cabbage: from 58 to 230 — up 297 percent. The whole basket went from 1,282 yen to 5,210 yen — up 306 percent. If we average those five product increases — 329, 165, 297, 209, and 297 — and divide by five, we get 259 percent. That is the average across products. It is not the average per year. To get the average inflation per year, use this equation: take the final total divided by the initial total, raise that to the power of one over the number of years, then subtract one. So 5,210 over 1,282, to the power of one over 24, minus one. That comes out to about 6 percent a year. So this was not one sudden jump. Over about 24 years, these prices rose by roughly 6 percent a year, on average.

## Is 6% the inflation rate?

Do you think 6 percent is the inflation rate? One shop. Two days. Five items. That is a story from a receipt. It is not yet the official number. And there is another problem. Not everyone buys the same things. Some people never buy milk. Some people never buy rice. So even with these five items, it is not obvious how they should be weighted. Whose basket is this? Hold that question. Let's see what the inflation rate really means.

## The Consumer Price Index

Three things. First, the definition. The CPI is the cost of a fixed basket of goods and services, relative to a base period. Same basket. Different dates. Second, the weights. A weight is that category's share of total spending: category spending divided by total spending. In Japan, food is about 28 percent, housing about 22 percent, transport about 14 percent, health about 5 percent. Other, about 32 percent, is everything else in the basket: education, clothing, fuel and utilities, furniture, culture and recreation, and miscellaneous spending. That is how the Statistics Bureau answers whose milk and whose rice. Items that take a bigger share of spending get a bigger weight. Third, the formula. Think of it this way: each price change is weighted by quantity. Q-zero is the base-period quantity — that is the weight. Those quantities are fixed. They come from the base basket, and they do not change over time. So we price the same basket today and in the base period, take the ratio, and multiply by one hundred. That is the CPI.

## Inflation, Deflation, and Disinflation

Look at this graph. Time runs left to right. The vertical axis is the CPI — the price level. Inflation measures the rate of change in the price level, so it is the first derivative of the CPI: the slope of this curve. Does inflation measure the rate of change in the inflation? That is the second derivative of the CPI — how fast inflation itself is accelerating or decelerating. On the left: inflation. The CPI is rising. The curve goes up steeply. Prices are higher than before. In the middle: deflation. The CPI is falling. The curve turns down. The price level itself is dropping. Many of you will think falling prices sound good. They are not. When people expect cheaper prices next year, they postpone spending. Companies then earn less revenue, so they stop hiring. Incomes fall. And that raises the risk of a recession. Japan lived with this for years: low inflation and, at times, falling prices — including all-items CPI of minus 0.2 percent in 2021 — and that is why the Bank of Japan targets 2 percent rather than zero. Last comes disinflation, which is about that second derivative. The CPI is still rising: the curve is still going up, so inflation is still positive. But each step up is smaller than the last. The slope is getting flatter: inflation is decelerating. Prices are still rising, just more slowly. That is not deflation. Deflation would require the curve to fall.
