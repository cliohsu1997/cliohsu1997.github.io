## Opening

Today we are going to walk through some questions about inflation. Simply put, inflation is how fast prices rise across the economy over time.

First: usually people think inflation is bad because their money buys less. The same cash gets you less than it used to. You see it in everyday prices — groceries climbing year after year, a bus ticket that costs more than last semester, student loan payments that feel heavier as living costs rise, rent that takes a bigger share of the paycheck. When prices across the board keep rising, your paycheck just doesn't go as far. That is why inflation feels unfair: you did nothing differently, but your money lost value.

Second: inflation can also have benefits. A little inflation can push people to invest instead of letting cash sit there doing nothing, and it can help the job market bring real wages down without painful pay cuts. So it's not only “inflation hurts.”

Third: if inflation is really costly, what can the central bank do to reduce it? We'll come back to that — what tools they have, and what those tools cost.

Let's start with something you have all seen: a receipt.

## The receipt

If we want to talk about rising prices, the easiest place to start is something you already know how to read. Look at the slide. On the left are two real receipts from the same supermarket in Hadano. May 12, 2002: JUSCO. October 12, 2026: AEON. Same store family — JUSCO was later renamed AEON. Same five items. Same quantities.

Now look at the table. Rice, five kilograms: 980 yen to 4,200 — up 329 percent. Milk, one litre: 98 to 260 — up 165 percent. Ten eggs: 78 to 310 — up 297 percent. One loaf of bread: 68 to 210 — up 209 percent. One cabbage: 58 to 230 — up 297 percent. The whole basket: 1,282 yen to 5,210 — up 306 percent.

Those are big total increases over a long stretch of time. If we average those five increases and divide by five, we get 259 percent. That is the average across products — not per year. For average inflation per year, take final over initial, raise to one over the number of years, then subtract one. So 5,210 over 1,282, to the power of one over 24, minus one — about 6 percent a year. Not one sudden jump. Over 24 years, roughly 6 percent a year on average.

## Is 6% the inflation rate?

So — do you think 6 percent is the inflation rate? One shop. Two days. Five items. That's just a story from a receipt. It's not the official number yet.

Here is another problem. Not everyone buys the same things, so not everyone felt the same rise. Look back at the table. Milk rose 165 percent. Rice rose 329 percent. If your basket is mainly milk, prices rose a lot less for you than if your basket is heavy in rice — or eggs, or cabbage. Bread sits in between. Same store. Same years. Different shopping lists — so different people feel inflation differently.

So even with only five items, different people feel different price rises. How should those items be weighted? Whose basket should we use — the milk drinker's, or the rice buyer's? That is exactly what the official inflation rate has to answer. And the answer is the Consumer Price Index.

## The Consumer Price Index

We just saw the problem: without a shared basket and shared weights, everyone has their own inflation story. The CPI is how we move from “whose milk and whose rice?” to one official number. Three things.

First, the definition. The CPI is the cost of a fixed basket of goods and services, compared to a base year. Same basket. Different dates. Not your personal shopping list, and not mine — one shared basket for the country. How is that basket chosen? From what households actually buy and spend. In Japan, the Statistics Bureau runs household expenditure surveys. They ask many households what they purchase, then build the basket and its weights from average spending in a base year. Right now that base is 2025. So the shared basket is an average shopping list for the country, not any one person's.

Second, the weights. Once you have a basket, you still need to decide how much each item counts. A weight is just category spending divided by total spending. In Japan: food about 28 percent, housing about 22, transport about 14, health about 5. Other, about 32 percent, covers the rest — education, clothing, fuel and utilities, furniture, culture and recreation, and miscellaneous. So the Bureau does not pick the milk drinker or the rice buyer. It uses average spending shares. Bigger share of spending, bigger weight. Milk and rice both matter — but only based on how much households actually spend on them.

Third, the formula. Now we put those ideas into one calculation. Look at the box on the slide. Each item j has a basket quantity, q-j — fixed from the survey. Think of two shopping trips with the exact same cart: same items, same quantities. That cart does not change. Only the prices change. In the denominator: what did that cart cost in the base year? Base-year prices times those fixed quantities, q-j. In the numerator: what does that same cart cost today? Today's prices times the same q-j. Divide today's cost by the base-year cost, multiply by one hundred — that is the CPI. If the CPI is 103, the same basket costs 3 percent more than in the base year.

## Inflation, Deflation, and Disinflation

So far we have a level — the CPI. But policymakers and newspapers usually talk about a rate. So the CPI is a level — just a number for how expensive the basket is right now. The inflation rate tells you how fast that level is changing, and in which direction. The formula is simple: this year's CPI minus last year's CPI, divided by last year's CPI. That percent change is the slope of the CPI curve on the graph.

Inflation means the CPI is rising. The rate is positive. Prices are higher than before. Deflation means the CPI is falling. The rate is negative. The price level itself is dropping. Disinflation means the CPI is still rising, but more slowly — the rate is still positive, just smaller than before. The slope is getting flatter.

Look at the graph. Time runs left to right. The vertical axis is the CPI — the price level. On the left: inflation. The CPI is rising. The curve goes up steeply. In the middle: deflation. The CPI is falling. The curve turns down. Falling prices sound good. They are not. When people expect cheaper prices next year, they wait to spend. Firms earn less, so they stop hiring. Incomes fall. And that makes a recession more likely. Japan lived with this for years: low inflation and, at times, falling prices — including all-items CPI of minus 0.2 percent in 2021. That is why the Bank of Japan targets 2 percent rather than zero. On the right: disinflation. The CPI is still rising, so inflation is still positive. But each step up is smaller than the last. Prices are still rising, just more slowly. That is not deflation. Deflation would require the curve itself to fall.

## Example: milk and rice

We've defined CPI and the inflation rate. Let's make this concrete with a tiny example so you can compute it yourself. Basket: two litres of milk and two bags of rice. Quantities stay fixed. Only prices change.

Here is the question. Compute the CPI at t-zero, t-one, and t-two. Then compute the inflation rate at t-one and at t-two. At t-two, the CPI rose again — is that deflation?

Here is the price table. Milk: 100 yen at the base, then 120, then 125. Rice: 200 at the base, then 240, then 250. Take a few seconds and work it out. [pause — then advance for the solution]

Solution. Basket cost equals two times the milk price plus two times the rice price. At the base, t-zero: two times 100 plus two times 200 — that is 200 plus 400 — 600 yen. We set CPI to 100. At t-one: two times 120 plus two times 240 — 240 plus 480 — 720. CPI equals one hundred times 720 over 600 — 120. At t-two: two times 125 plus two times 250 — 250 plus 500 — 750. CPI equals one hundred times 750 over 600 — 125.

Now the inflation rates. At t-one: 120 minus 100, over 100 — 20 percent. At t-two: 125 minus 120, over 120 — about 4.2 percent. The CPI rose from 100 to 120 to 125, so both periods are inflation — the price level is still going up. But the inflation rate fell from 20 percent to about 4.2 percent. Prices are still rising, just more slowly. That is disinflation — not deflation. Deflation would require the CPI itself to fall.

## Three costs of inflation

We have measured inflation. Before we ask what causes it, is inflation only bad? Remember the opening: people hate inflation because their paycheck doesn't go as far. Economists go further and name specific costs. Start with three of them.

Cost one: shoe-leather costs — the hassle of holding cash. Think about what happens when prices keep rising. Cash in your wallet loses value. So people hold less cash and make more trips to the bank or ATM. They spend time managing money instead of doing something useful. Economists call this the shoe-leather cost — like wearing out your shoes going back and forth to the bank. Even moderate inflation makes people waste time and energy just trying to protect the value of their money.

Cost two: menu costs — the cost of changing prices. From the firm's side, rising prices are not free to update. When inflation is high, businesses have to change prices more often. That means printing new menus, updating websites, re-pricing shelves, and telling customers. These are called menu costs. There is also a second problem: price confusion. If some businesses change prices quickly and others lag behind, it becomes harder for everyone to tell what things are really worth. That makes buying, selling, and planning harder for everyone.

Cost three: unexpected inflation moves wealth around — and creates uncertainty. This is the most important cost, and the one that catches people off guard. Loans and pensions are usually written in fixed yen or dollars. When inflation is higher or lower than people expected, it quietly moves wealth from one group to another.

Here is how it works with loans. Suppose you borrow 10,000 dollars to buy a car. The bank charges you 5 percent interest because it expects inflation to be 2 percent. That means the bank expects to earn a real return of about 3 percent — a fair deal. But if inflation actually turns out to be 6 percent, the bank only earns about minus 1 percent after inflation. You, the borrower, win because you pay back the loan with dollars that are worth less. The bank loses. The opposite happens if inflation is lower than expected.

A real-world example: in 1960, a typical mortgage had an interest rate of about 6 percent. People expected inflation to be around 2.5 percent, so the bank expected to earn about 3.5 percent after inflation. But actual inflation over the life of that mortgage averaged 5 percent. So the bank only earned about 1 percent after inflation. The borrower got a great deal — at the bank's expense.

The same thing happens with pensions. If you are retired and living on a fixed pension, higher-than-expected inflation hurts you. Your pension buys less, and you cannot do anything about it. You are like the bank in the mortgage example — you get paid back in dollars that are worth less.

The bigger problem is uncertainty. When inflation is unpredictable, nobody knows what to expect. Borrowers and lenders have to guess. Workers and retirees have to guess. Businesses have to guess. And most people do not like uncertainty — they are risk-averse. So unpredictable inflation makes everyone worse off, even if they end up on the winning side by accident. It is like playing a game where the rules keep changing — you might win, but you cannot plan, and that is stressful and costly.

## Benefits of inflation

So inflation is not only costs. If it were only bad, central banks would aim for zero. There are also benefits. Two of them are on the slide.

First: modest inflation can encourage investment. Start from cash. When inflation is low but positive, cash sitting in a checking account loses a little value each year. That pushes people to invest in things that grow — stocks, equipment, new businesses. Some economists argue that a little inflation pushes money into real investments instead of letting it sit still. This is not an argument for high inflation. But it is one reason why some central banks aim for a small positive inflation rate instead of zero.

Second: a little inflation helps the job market. Here is the friction. Companies rarely cut workers’ pay. Workers hate pay cuts. But sometimes the economy needs real wages to fall — for example, when demand for a certain type of worker drops. With zero inflation, the only way to cut real wages is a pay cut in dollar terms, which is hard to do. With a little inflation — say 2 or 3 percent — real wages can fall gradually without anyone taking a pay cut. A 2 percent pay cut with no inflation feels terrible. But a 3 percent raise with 5 percent inflation feels like a raise — even though buying power went down. So a little inflation can “grease the wheels” of the job market and help keep unemployment lower.

We will not focus on seigniorage today — revenue from creating money — even though it sometimes shows up in textbook lists of benefits.

## Hyperinflation

So far we have talked about ordinary inflation — a few percent a year, or even the higher rates after COVID. There is a much more extreme case. When prices rise so fast that they can double in hours or days, economists call that hyperinflation.

The clearest modern example is Zimbabwe. In 2008, inflation exploded. At the peak, prices were rising so fast that people watched the value of cash collapse almost in real time. Savings in the local currency were wiped out. People stopped trusting the Zimbabwean dollar. They turned to foreign currency — mainly the U.S. dollar — and to barter. The local money stopped doing its job.

Why did it get that bad? The government printed money to cover deficits. Once people lost confidence, they tried to spend cash as fast as they could, before it lost value again — and that made prices rise even faster.

And this is not only history. As of recent 2026 accounting updates, Argentina and Venezuela are still treated as hyperinflationary economies. Zimbabwe introduced a new currency after abandoning the old dollar, but it is still watched closely — the problem of losing trust in money does not disappear overnight. That is why central banks care so much about keeping inflation under control.

## Tools against inflation

We have seen that inflation has costs — and in the extreme, hyperinflation. So what can policy do about it? The key framing is this: which tool you reach for depends on how costly inflation is, and later we will see that it also depends on the cause. Start with three tools.

### Tool 1: Interest rates

The tool you hear about most is the interest rate. Think of it as the price of borrowing. If the central bank raises rates, loans get more expensive. Households and firms borrow less, spend less, and invest less. Demand cools, and that can bring inflation down. If the central bank lowers rates, borrowing gets cheaper, spending tends to rise, and inflation pressure can go up. There is a tradeoff: fighting inflation with higher rates often means slower growth. That is the cost of using this tool hard.

### Tool 2: Yield curve control — Japan's case

A second tool is the price of long-term money. Short-term rates are not the whole story. Long-term rates matter for mortgages, business investment, and government borrowing. Yield curve control means the central bank targets those long rates too. Japan's Bank of Japan is the famous case. When short rates were already near zero, the BoJ bought government bonds as needed to keep long-term yields near a target. That kept long-term borrowing cheap across the curve. So even when the usual short-rate tool had little room left, Japan tried to keep stimulus flowing through long rates.

### Tool 3: Reserve requirements

A third tool works through quantity — how much money banks can create. Banks do not lend out every yen or dollar they take in. Regulators can require them to keep a share of deposits as reserves — cash sitting idle. If you raise that reserve ratio, banks must hold more and can lend less. Credit tightens, money creation slows, demand cools, and inflation pressure falls. If you lower the ratio, banks can lend more, credit expands, and spending can rise. The idea is simple: control credit at the source — how much banks are allowed to create.

We now know the toolkit. Next: before you choose a tool, you need to know what is driving inflation.

## Diagnosing the cause

Three channels — demand, supply, and expectations. I'll use the United States as examples.

First, demand. During COVID, a lot of manufacturing was shut down. Factories closed. That disrupted supply. Then people and the government tried to stimulate the economy by piping money into it — that is called fiscal stimulus. One form was sending cash directly to households — “helicopter money.” People received money in their accounts. Spending jumped faster than firms could supply. Too much spending chasing goods — prices get pulled up. So it was that combination: supply already tight from the shutdowns, then a big demand push from the stimulus.

Second, supply. Demand is not the only path. Sometimes costs rise first. This is the cost side. An oil shock — a war in the Persian Gulf, or a threat to the Strait of Hormuz — makes energy scarce and expensive. Oil feeds into transport, plastics, heating, almost everything. A trade war belongs here too: countries raise tariffs and disrupt shipping, so imports and production get more expensive. Same idea — supply is tight or more expensive — so prices are pushed up even without a demand boom.

Third, expectations. Even after a shock starts to fade, people still form beliefs about what comes next. Suppose inflation is already high. Workers expect prices to keep rising, so they demand higher wages. Firms pass those labor costs into prices. Inflation stays high, expectations rise again, and the loop continues. That wage–price spiral can turn a short-term shock into inflation that sticks around.

## The output--inflation tradeoff

So different causes need different responses — even with the same toolkit. We already saw interest rates as a tool. Here is why the cause matters when you use them.

If inflation is mainly from hot demand — helicopter money — cooling demand can be the right move. Raise rates. Borrowing becomes more expensive. Spending and investment cool. Inflation comes down. The cost is slower growth. That is the classic output–inflation tradeoff: lower inflation, but weaker output.

But if inflation is from a supply shock — oil, Hormuz, trade war — the picture is different. Remember: costs are already pushing prices up, and firms are already cutting production because inputs are scarce or expensive. So you can get prices up and output down at the same time. We call that stagflation: stagnation plus inflation.

If policymakers then fight that inflation only by cutting demand hard — as if it were the COVID stimulus case — they deepen the slump while energy costs are still high. So the lesson is not “never cool demand.” It is: figure out the cause first. Demand-driven inflation and supply-driven inflation are not the same problem — and treating a Hormuz-style oil shock as if it were only hot demand is how you walk into stagflation.

## Case study: Japan through the CPI chart

Now Japan — the economy you live in, and one of the longest inflation experiments in modern history. This line chart is Japan's annual CPI inflation rate, from the World Bank series on FRED. Above zero: prices rising. Below zero: deflation — the price level itself is falling. Six chapters. About three to five minutes. Background first, then the claim.

Chapter one: the bubble peak around 1990. Background first. In 1985, under the Plaza Accord, the U.S. pushed Japan to let the yen appreciate — to shrink America's large trade deficit. The yen surged. Japanese exports fell. Recession risk rose. So the BOJ cut rates hard to cushion the economy. Cheap credit lasted for years and fueled land and stock speculation. How extreme? At the peak, the Imperial Palace grounds — about three-quarters of a square mile — were estimated worth about the same as all the land in California. Japanese land overall was valued at about five times U.S. land. And Tokyo real estate was said to top the entire United States. Then the BOJ hiked from 1989 over inflation and overheating worries. Stocks peaked at end-1989 and crashed in 1990. Land prices peaked later and kept falling. On the chart, CPI inflation is still around 3 percent in 1990–91 — consumer prices had not yet fallen — but the asset boom was already breaking. Firms and banks that had borrowed against high land values suddenly looked much weaker. That balance-sheet damage is the background for everything that follows.

Chapter two: the long near-zero and negative stretch. After the bubble, demand stayed weak. Bad loans piled up. People expected flat or falling prices, so they waited to spend. On the chart, CPI inflation turns negative in 1995 and hugs zero from below for years — the deflation era. The BOJ kept expanding its toolkit: zero rates in 1999, QE in 2001, then QQE in 2013, a negative policy rate in 2016, and yield curve control the same year. Remember Tool 2? That is this chapter.

Chapter three: the 2008–09 dip. Brief bump in 2008, then a sharp drop — about minus 1.4 percent in 2009. Why? Oil and energy had surged, then crashed after the global financial crisis, and demand weakened. So the chart also moves with global commodity swings, not only Japan-only stories.

Chapter four: the 2013–14 uptick — Abenomics and the tax hike. April 2013: QQE and a 2 percent price target. On the chart, 2014 jumps to about 2.7 or 2.8 percent. But be careful. The Statistics Bureau and the BOJ both note that much of that jump was the April 2014 consumption-tax hike from 5 percent to 8 percent. Strip out the tax, and underlying inflation stayed well below 2 percent. The line moves up — but partly from a tax change, not only new inflation psychology.

Chapter five: the COVID dip. 2020 is about zero. 2021 is about minus 0.2 percent on the all-items CPI. The pandemic lull pulled prices down again, just when Japan had spent years trying to push them up.

Chapter six: the 2022-and-after pickup. From April 2022, core CPI excluding fresh food stayed above the BOJ's 2 percent target. Headline peaked near 4.3 percent in early 2023 and averaged around 3.3 percent that year. Drivers: first a weaker yen and higher imported energy and goods costs; then stronger wages — the 2024 spring talks delivered the largest pay hikes in decades. March 2024: the BOJ ended negative rates and YCC, and set the short-rate target at 0 to 0.1 percent. Same chart, new chapter: Japan spent decades fighting deflation. Now the question is how to manage inflation that has finally returned.

So do not just say “Japan had deflation.” Read the periods: bubble, long flat line, crisis dip, tax-and-QQE bump, COVID dip, then the recent turnaround. Each move has a background — that is how you diagnose inflation in the real world.
