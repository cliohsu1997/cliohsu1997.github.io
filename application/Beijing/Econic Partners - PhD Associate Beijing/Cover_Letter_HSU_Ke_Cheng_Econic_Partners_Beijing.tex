My research works with large proprietary datasets to study welfare and run counterfactuals. In ``Too Much Unsold Stock?,'' joint with Kei Kawai (Senior Consultant, Compass Lexecon) and Kohei Kawaguchi (Advisor, UTokyo Economic Consulting Inc.), we ask whether resale price maintenance is pro-competitive by encouraging sellers to carry inventory, and how much unsold stock is an environmental cost of price commitment. Estimating a dynamic oligopoly model on monthly bookstore data, we find that the classical static model overstates marginal cost by about 20\% once dynamic demand uncertainty and inventory decisions are ignored. Working on this project taught me to let the data speak first---cleaning and transforming raw records, then using descriptive statistics, regression, and clear figures to establish the key facts before turning to structural models.

My job market paper studies return policy as a trade-off between purchase and return efficiency: a stricter return policy curbs inefficient ex-post returns, but the resulting lower return rates make higher prices more attractive for sellers and can exclude ex-ante efficient purchases. Automatic provision of return-shipping insurance does better in high-margin categories by expanding valuable purchases, while selective adoption outperforms automatic provision by 15--30 percentage points of first-best welfare in thinner-margin categories by curbing excessive returns. A partial return-penalty discount (a discounted price plus a 10--15\% restocking fee) beats both outside dresses, reaching 75--78\% of first-best welfare. I build reproducible pipelines on AWS and HPC/SLURM in Python, R, and C++, and write tests to keep the code clear and correct. In Ph.D.-level IO coursework, I also ran merger simulations, entry/exit counterfactuals, and firm-conduct identification exercises.
