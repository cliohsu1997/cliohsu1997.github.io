Teaching is most fulfilling when a student who was stuck begins to see why an economic outcome occurs. To help students make that shift, I want them to use economic models not simply to solve equations, but to distinguish an observed pattern from the mechanism that produces it. For example, when a new restaurant opens on campus, students may treat a long line as evidence that the food is good. However, the line is only a signal. As more people observe it and join, the line can keep growing even when the food is bad. Thus, this familiar example gives students a way into herding under imperfect information. A different example comes from asking why a government might issue consumption vouchers instead of cash. Because vouchers must be spent, more of the transfer enters current demand and the multiplier is larger. These examples shape how I teach. I begin with the economic intuition and introduce the formula only after students understand the question it answers. Once the intuition is clear, I ask students to work through the reasoning and tell me where it stops being clear. When one student asks a question, it often helps several others and shows me where I need to explain an idea more simply. For me, finding that clearer explanation is the part of teaching I enjoy most.

My teaching experience at the Hong Kong University of Science and Technology has taught me how to put this approach into practice. From 2020 to 2025, I served as a teaching assistant for undergraduate macroeconomics, industrial organization, and the economics of uncertainty and information. Across these courses, I led weekly tutorials, held office hours, and graded. Through tutorials and office hours, I learned to identify where a student's reasoning became unclear and rebuild the argument from that point. At the doctoral level, I primarily led tutorials and answered questions for PhD microeconomic theory, which is taken by doctoral students across the Business School. In this course, I often approached the same question in several ways until the idea became clear. My evaluations provide one measure of this work. On a scale where 4 means good and 5 means extremely satisfied, my average across these teaching assistant appointments was 4.48. My two evaluations for PhD microeconomic theory were 4.85 and 4.83. In addition, I lectured in the mathematics camp for the MSc in Economics in 2026. Because most participants did not have the mathematical background assumed by the program, I developed the intuition and notation step by step. My teaching received a score of 4.5 out of 5, and student comments included ``Very nice and patient!'' and ``Excellent teaching.'' Alongside my classroom teaching, I served as Teaching Assistant Coordinator for the Business School from 2025 to 2026. In that role, I helped postgraduate students design teaching materials, evaluated their mock teaching, and discussed where students might lose the thread and how those steps could be explained more clearly.

As the instructor of my own courses, I would keep the same emphasis on intuition, active reasoning, and clear explanation. I am prepared to teach microeconomic theory at the undergraduate, master's, and PhD levels. Beyond microeconomic theory, I can teach industrial organization, information economics, empirical industrial organization, and mathematics for economists through the master's level. At the undergraduate level, I can also teach macroeconomics and econometrics. I would also like to help students use AI responsibly in research projects. Since AI can produce confident but incorrect output when a task has not been defined carefully, students should first state the problem and decide what evidence will show that the task is complete. After that, I would teach them practical habits for managing the work. These include reporting their progress, maintaining readable code in R or Python, and using Git to track versions. Together, these habits help students catch mistakes and verify work produced with AI.
