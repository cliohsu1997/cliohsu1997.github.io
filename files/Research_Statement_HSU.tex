\begin{center}
{\Large Research Statement}\\[0.3em]
HSU Ke Cheng
\end{center}

My research lies in digital economics and industrial organization. My work centers on one theme: how a decision made before uncertainty is resolved interacts with the adjustment that follows. An agent must commit before the uncertainty is resolved, and adjusting afterward is costly, so anticipating that cost changes the earlier decision and the two are determined together. The theme splits into two streams: consumers face uncertainty about match value, and sellers face uncertainty about demand.

Consumers can resolve the match before paying, by searching, or after paying, by returning. Between search and purchase, product design shapes the distribution of match value and directs which consumers search which products, a margin I study with Professor AU Pak Hung. Between purchase and return, a return policy sets the cost of returning, and the return rate feeds back into the price, tying purchase efficiency to return efficiency. With Ce Bian and Liang Zhao, I follow the same feedback into pass-through and incidence. A calculation that keeps only the purchase margin can reverse the welfare conclusion.

For sellers, inventory is chosen before demand is realized. If demand exceeds inventory, the product stocks out. If inventory exceeds demand, the seller returns the unsold units. With Professor Kohei Kawaguchi and Professor Kei Kawai, I study resale price maintenance in Japanese publishing. The retail price is set in advance, so unsold copies are returned upstream and discarded, at an environmental cost. The paragraphs below describe these papers, and then the questions I plan to take up next.

\textbf{Job Market Paper}

In my job market paper, ``Designing Return Policy in E-Commerce: A Trade-off between Purchase and Return Efficiency,'' I study the effect of return policies by asking whether they encourage efficient returns and purchases. A stricter return policy curbs inefficient ex-post returns, but the resulting lower return rates make higher product prices more attractive for sellers, leading to possible exclusion of ex-ante efficient purchases. I compare automatic coverage that reimburses return-shipping costs (RI) with optional coverage (CI) by estimating a structural model of purchase, insurance, and return decisions under monopoly pricing across six apparel categories. A lenient return policy like RI performs better in high-margin, high-salvage categories by expanding valuable purchases, whereas a strict return policy like CI performs better in thinner-margin categories by curbing excessive returns. Partial RPD, a menu pairing a discounted price with a medium restocking fee as penalty, performs better than RI and CI for most categories by better balancing purchase and return efficiency.

\textbf{Other Papers}

In ``Pass-Through and Incidence with Product Returns,'' we study pass-through and incidence with product returns. In contrast to the case without returns, a tax such as a platform commission fee or a sales tax can create negative pass-through and improve social welfare. When a high price significantly raises consumers' return probability, the seller reduces the quantity supplied because of a lower effective margin. This downward-sloping supply curve can lower the equilibrium price in response to a tax increase, raising social surplus by reducing the return rate.

In ``Product Design with Heterogeneous Search Costs,'' we build on Wolinsky's (1986) sequential search model and introduce search-cost heterogeneity. Search costs shape not only whether consumers search but which products they search. In equilibrium, low-search-cost consumers seek niche products and high-search-cost consumers seek standardized products. The main result is that a fall in search costs can raise both prices when the endogenous shift in which product to search dominates the search-encouraging effect.

In ``Too Much Unsold Stock?: Environmental Consequences of Price Commitment,'' we study resale price maintenance in the Japanese publishing industry, where publishers set retail prices and unsold books are returned upstream and discarded. Resale price maintenance is procompetitive because it encourages sellers to carry inventory in the low-demand state. When realized demand falls short of that inventory, however, the seller cannot lower the price to clear the market, so unsold units pile up at an environmental cost. We build a dynamic oligopoly model with demand uncertainty, estimate it on monthly bookstore data, and ask how much unsold stock is due to price commitment and how a deposit-refund tax can reduce waste.

\textbf{Future Research Agenda}

\textbf{Search, purchase, and return jointly.} My two consumer-side papers treat search and return in isolation, but search resolves match value only partially, so the two margins interact. I plan to study search, purchase, and return jointly: how search technology such as personal recommendation changes return behavior, and how the return policy changes how consumers search.

\textbf{Bracketing, substitutes, and complements.} Allowing consumers to buy products of different sizes or colors creates a bracketing issue: a consumer orders several variants, keeps only one, and returns the rest, generating a large return loss. Bracketing is a special case of substitutes, so I plan to study whether a policy that encourages the purchase of complements can solve the issue. Encouraging complements makes demand less elastic and strengthens surplus extraction, but returning one product makes the consumer more likely to return the other, so the seller can offer higher quality or a lower price to deter returns, partially offsetting that incentive.

\textbf{Return policy on a two-sided platform.} A return policy changes which consumers join the platform and how each returns, which changes how much a seller can earn and in turn which sellers participate and what prices they set. I plan to study how composition on one side feeds into the other, and how return policy should be designed taking the cross-side network effect into account.
